# Menu Engineering: A Comprehensive Study for Restaurants

## Executive Summary

Menu engineering is a data-driven approach that combines food cost analysis, sales data analytics, and menu design psychology to maximize restaurant profitability. This systematic discipline evaluates every menu item based on two key dimensions: **profitability (contribution margin)** and **popularity (sales volume)**. Restaurants that apply menu engineering principles consistently see a **10-15% increase in profits** within 90 days of implementation, with some achieving up to 35% revenue increases through optimized menu design.

## 1. What is Menu Engineering?

### Definition

**Menu engineering** is the systematic practice of analyzing each item on a restaurant menu by two dimensions—**profitability** (how much money the restaurant makes per sale) and **popularity** (how often it sells)—then redesigning the menu to strategically guide customers toward high-profit items without them realizing it.

It is not about tricking customers; rather, it's about making profitable dishes easy to find, appealing, and hard to resist while managing or removing underperforming items.

### Core Purpose

The primary goals of menu engineering include:

- **Maximize profitability** per guest without raising prices across the board
- **Optimize menu design** to influence customer ordering behavior
- **Identify and promote** high-margin items
- **Improve or remove** low-performing dishes
- **Create a balanced menu** that attracts guests while increasing revenue

### Why It Matters

The average restaurant profit margin is between **3-5%** in the UK and **5-7%** in the US. With such thin margins, every decision about menu items has a significant impact on the bottom line. Menu engineering provides a scientific, data-driven framework to make these decisions rather than relying on gut feeling.

> *"Your menu is your single most important sales tool. Not your Instagram, not your Yelp reviews, not your server's recommendations. Your menu."* — MenuStack

## 2. History and Origins

Menu engineering as a formal discipline was developed in the **1980s** by two researchers at **Michigan State University**:

- **Professor Donald I. Smith** (often called "Coach" Smith)
- **Michael L. Kasavana**

They adapted the **Boston Consulting Group's Growth-Share Matrix** for the restaurant industry, creating a framework that has remained the standard for over 40 years.

The concept was formally published in **1990** and has been refined continuously since then, with modern implementations incorporating digital menu optimization, POS data integration, and advanced analytics.

## 3. The Menu Engineering Matrix

The menu engineering matrix is the foundational tool that categorizes every menu item based on two axes:

| | **Low Popularity** | **High Popularity** |
|---|---|---|
| **High Profit** | 🧩 **Puzzles** | ⭐ **Stars** |
| **Low Profit** | 🐶 **Dogs** | 🐴 **Plow Horses** |

---

### 3.1 Stars — High Profit + High Popularity

**Definition:** Items that generate strong contribution margins AND sell well.

**Characteristics:**
- Your best-performing dishes
- Customer favorites that also make you money
- Typically 15-25% of menu items

**Strategy: RETAIN & PROTECT**

- Feature prominently on the menu (Golden Triangle positions)
- Maintain exact recipe specifications—don't compromise quality
- Use in marketing and server recommendations
- Consider modest price increases (customers who love it are less price-sensitive)
- Never discount these items
- Monitor ingredient costs closely

**Example:** A Truffle Burger at $22 with 68% margin that sells 312 units in 90 days.

---

### 3.2 Puzzles — High Profit + Low Popularity

**Definition:** Items with excellent margins but low sales volume. The product is good—the marketing is bad.

**Characteristics:**
- Hidden profit opportunities
- Often specialty or premium items
- Need visibility boosts
- Typically 15-20% of menu items

**Strategy: REPLATE (Increase Visibility)**

- Move to high-visibility positions (top of section, Golden Triangle)
- Add photos (items with photos sell 30% more)
- Improve descriptions with sensory language
- Train servers to recommend specifically
- Consider renaming with more appealing titles
- Run as limited-time specials to test demand
- Slightly lower price if margin allows

**Example:** Pan-Seared Duck at $26 with 74% margin but only 87 orders in 90 days.

---

### 3.3 Plow Horses — Low Profit + High Popularity

**Definition:** Items that sell very well but don't generate strong margins. Customers love them, but you're barely making money.

**Characteristics:**
- Often comfort food staples or "classic" items
- Frequently priced too low relative to food cost
- May be driving customer loyalty
- Typically 25-35% of menu items

**Strategy: REPRICE (Improve Margins)**

- Increase price by $1-2 gradually (test carefully)
- Reduce portion size slightly (10% reduction often goes unnoticed)
- Substitute cheaper ingredients where quality impact is minimal
- Bundle with high-margin sides or drinks
- Move to less prominent menu positions
- Add premium upcharge options (e.g., add protein for $5)
- Pair with high-margin beverages

**Example:** Classic Cheeseburger at $14 with 42% margin but 445 orders in 90 days.

---

### 3.4 Dogs — Low Profit + Low Popularity

**Definition:** Items that neither sell well nor generate meaningful profit.

**Characteristics:**
- Taking up valuable menu space
- Adding kitchen complexity
- Contributing almost nothing to the bottom line
- Typically 15-25% of menu items

**Strategy: RETHINK (Remove or Reinvent)**

- Remove entirely if no strategic purpose
- Hide at bottom of section with minimal description
- Consider replacing with new Puzzle candidates
- Evaluate if needed for dietary restrictions or variety
- Check if acting as price anchor (may justify keeping)
- Rework recipe and reprice if there's potential

**Example:** Veggie Wrap at $11 with 38% margin and only 54 orders in 90 days.

## 4. Key Metrics and Calculations

### 4.1 Contribution Margin

The most important profitability metric in menu engineering:

```
Contribution Margin = Selling Price - Food Cost (Plate Cost)
```

**Example:**
- Dish sells for $18
- Food cost (ingredients) = $5
- Contribution Margin = $18 - $5 = $13

> **Note:** Use contribution margin (dollar value), NOT food cost percentage. A dish with 35% food cost but $24 contribution margin is more valuable than one with 20% food cost but $6 margin.

---

### 4.2 Food Cost Percentage

```
Food Cost % = (Plate Cost ÷ Menu Price) × 100
```

**Example:**
- Plate cost: $5.00
- Menu price: $18.00
- Food Cost % = ($5.00 ÷ $18.00) × 100 = 27.8%

**Target range:** 28-32% for most restaurants

---

### 4.3 Menu Item Popularity

```
Menu Item Popularity % = (Items Sold ÷ Total Items Sold) × 100
```

**Example:**
- Chicken Parmesan sold: 200 times
- Total sandwiches sold: 550
- Popularity = (200 ÷ 550) × 100 = 36.3%

---

### 4.4 Average Thresholds

To categorize items on the matrix:

| Metric | Calculation | Purpose |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Average Contribution Margin** | Sum of all CM ÷ Number of items | Divides "high" and "low" profit |
| **Average Popularity** | (100% ÷ Number of items) × 0.7 | Popularity threshold (70% of equal distribution) |

---

### 4.5 Profit Margin

```
Profit Margin % = (Profit per Dish ÷ Menu Price) × 100
```

**Example:**
- Profit per dish: $7.50
- Menu price: $14.00
- Profit Margin = ($7.50 ÷ $14.00) × 100 = 53.6%

## 5. Step-by-Step Menu Engineering Process

### Step 1: Define Your Time Period

- **Standard:** 90 days of sales data
- **Seasonal analysis:** Same 3-month period from previous year
- **Quick check:** 30 days for fast-moving data
- **Avoid:** Periods with unusual events, promotions, or anomalies

---

### Step 2: Gather Sales Data

From your **POS system**, extract:

- Units sold per item
- Total revenue per item
- Sales by daypart (lunch vs. dinner)
- Server-level sales data (for training purposes)

---

### Step 3: Calculate Contribution Margins

For each menu item:

1. List all ingredients
2. Calculate cost per ingredient (bulk price ÷ portion yield)
3. Sum all ingredient costs = **Plate Cost**
4. Subtract from menu price = **Contribution Margin**

**Include:**
- Main ingredients
- Garnishes and sauces
- Accompaniments (fries, bread, etc.)
- Spices and seasonings
- Cooking oils

---

### Step 4: Calculate Averages

- **Average Contribution Margin** = Total CM ÷ Number of items
- **Average Popularity** = Total units sold ÷ Number of items

These averages become your dividing lines on the matrix.

---

### Step 5: Plot and Categorize

| Category | Contribution Margin | Popularity |
|----------|---------------------|------------|
| Stars | Above Average | Above Average |
| Puzzles | Above Average | Below Average |
| Plow Horses | Below Average | Above Average |
| Dogs | Below Average | Below Average |

---

### Step 6: Develop Category Strategies

Apply the **4 R's Framework**:

| Category | Action | Description |
|----------|--------|-------------|
| **Stars** | RETAIN | Protect, feature, maintain quality |
| **Puzzles** | REPLATE | Increase visibility, rename, reposition |
| **Plow Horses** | REPRICE | Adjust price, portions, ingredients |
| **Dogs** | RETHINK | Remove, reinvent, or replace |

---

### Step 7: Implement Changes

Make 2-3 changes at a time:

1. **Week 1-2:** Rewrite descriptions for top 5 high-margin items
2. **Week 3:** Rearrange menu placement (Stars/Puzzles to prime spots)
3. **Week 4:** Test price adjustments on Plow Horses
4. **Week 5:** Remove Dogs or replace with new candidates

---

### Step 8: Measure and Iterate

Re-analyze after **90 days**:

- Compare sales mix
- Track average check size
- Monitor food cost percentage
- Evaluate customer satisfaction

## 6. Menu Psychology and Design

### 6.1 The Golden Triangle

Eye-tracking studies reveal a predictable pattern in how customers scan menus:

```
           ┌─────────┐
           │  Upper  │
        ┌──│  Left   │
        │  └─────────┘
        │    ┌─────┐
   Upper│    │     │
   Right│ Center│
        │    └─────┘
        │
        └─────────────
        
Order of attention: Center → Upper Right → Upper Left
```

**Strategy:** Place your highest-margin items (Stars and Puzzles) in these positions.

---

### 6.2 Anchoring Effect

Place your **most expensive item** at the top of each category section.

- A $38 steak makes a $24 salmon feel reasonable
- The expensive item doesn't need to sell well—its job is to make others look like better value
- This is pure behavioral economics

---

### 6.3 Boxing and Highlighting

- Items inside a **box, shaded area, or with icons** receive 2-3x more attention
- Use for Stars and Puzzles—never for Plow Horses or Dogs
- Examples: "Chef's Favorite," "Most Popular," "House Special"

---

### 6.4 Descriptive Language

Research from **Cornell University** found that descriptive menu labels **increased sales by 27%**.

**Sensory words to use:**

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| Texture | Crispy, silky, tender, velvety, crunchy |
| Temperature | Sizzling, chilled, warm, ice-cold |
| Cooking | Wood-fired, slow-roasted, cedar-plank, braised |
| Origin | Heritage, line-caught, grass-fed, San Marzano |

**Compare:**

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| "Grilled salmon with rice" | "Wild-caught Atlantic salmon, cedar-plank grilled and finished with a honey-miso glaze" |
| "Chicken pasta" | "Hand-pulled fettuccine in a slow-simmered San Marzano cream sauce with herb-crusted chicken" |

---

### 6.5 Photography

- Items with photos sell **30% more** than items without
- Use sparingly—1-2 per page maximum
- Too many photos signals "low-end" dining
- Reserve for high-margin Puzzles you want to promote

---

### 6.6 Color Psychology

| Color | Effect |
|-------|--------|
| Red & Yellow | Stimulate appetite, good for callouts |
| Green | Signals freshness, health |
| Orange | Energetic, stimulates hunger |
| Blue | Suppresses appetite (avoid for food) |
| White Space | Communicates premium quality |

---

### 6.7 The Decoy Strategy

Place a slightly overpriced item next to your target item:

- Goal: Sell the $22 pasta (your Star)
- Place a $26 pasta next to it (the decoy)
- The $26 makes the $22 feel like smart value
- The decoy doesn't need to sell—it just makes the Star sell more

## 7. Pricing Psychology

### 7.1 Remove Dollar Signs

**Cornell University study:** Removing the "$" symbol increases average spending by **8%**.

| Don't Use | Use Instead |
|-----------|-------------|
| "$24.00" | "24" or "twenty-four" |
| "$14.99" | "15" for fine dining |

The dollar sign activates "the pain of paying"—remove it to reduce price sensitivity.

---

### 7.2 Avoid Price Columns

When prices are aligned in a column, customers:
- Scan down the column
- Pick the cheapest option
- Ignore your carefully crafted descriptions

**Solution:** Nest prices at the end of descriptions, after dots or spaces.

---

### 7.3 Charm Pricing vs. Round Numbers

| Restaurant Type | Recommended Style |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Fast Casual / Casual | Charm pricing: $12.95, $9.99 |
| Fine Dining / Upscale | Round numbers: $24, $42 |
| Coffee Shops | Modern round: $5, $6 |

- **.95 / .99** signals value/discount
- **Round numbers** signal premium quality

---

### 7.4 Price Increases on Stars

Stars can often handle **$0.50-$1.00 increases** without affecting volume:

- Customers who consistently order it demonstrate strong preference
- Price-sensitive customers have already self-selected out
- Small increases across multiple Stars = significant annual impact

**Example:**
- Star selling 40 units/day
- $0.75 price increase
- Additional annual profit: $10,950

## 9. Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 9.1 Using Food Cost Percentage Instead of Contribution Margin

| Wrong Approach | Correct Approach |
|----------------|------------------|
| Focus on 25% food cost | Focus on $13 contribution margin |
| Avoid high-cost items | Embrace high-margin items regardless of % |
| Miss the real picture | See actual dollars per sale |

**Example:**
- $10 item at 25% food cost = $7.50 margin
- $30 item at 35% food cost = $19.50 margin
- The $30 item is 2.8x more valuable despite higher %

---

### 9.2 Ignoring Seasonality

- Update menu **quarterly** or seasonally
- Take advantage of lower ingredient costs when in season
- Offer fresh, appealing dishes that match customer expectations
- Keep menu exciting and relevant

---

### 9.3 Prioritizing Profit Over Popularity

- Don't remove Plow Horses immediately—they drive traffic
- Instead: adjust portions, prices, or bundle with high-margin items
- Consider customer loyalty impact

---

### 9.4 Overcomplicating Data

**Focus on essentials:**
1. Food cost per dish
2. Sales volume
3. Contribution margin

Avoid analysis paralysis—start with your top 20 items.

---

### 9.5 Overcrowding the Menu

**Research from Culinary Institute of America:**
- Menus with **more than 7-10 items per category** cause decision fatigue
- Customers default to cheapest familiar option
- Fewer choices = faster decisions + focused demand

**Benefits of shorter menus:**
- Reduced kitchen complexity
- Lower inventory costs
- Less food waste
- Easier quality control

---

### 9.6 Not Training Staff

- Best menu = failed if servers don't recommend correctly
- Train on specific scripts for Puzzles
- Encourage upselling of Stars
- Create incentive programs

**Example script:**
> *"The lobster risotto is incredible tonight—chef uses a 45-minute reduction that makes the sauce unbelievable."*

---

### 9.7 One-Time Exercise

- Menu engineering is a **quarterly discipline**
- Ingredient costs change
- Customer preferences shift
- New competitors emerge
- Run analysis at least every 6 months, ideally quarterly

A well-balanced menu typically has:

| Category | Target % |
|----------|----------|

### 10.3 Beverage Menu

Don't forget drinks!

- Beverages typically carry **75-85% margins**
- A $9 cocktail costing $1.80 = $7.20 margin
- Often higher margin than your best entrée
- Apply same engineering framework

---

### 10.4 Multi-Location Considerations

- Run analysis per location
- A Star in downtown may be a Puzzle in suburbs
- Adapt menu strategy to local preferences
- Centralized reporting for franchise/group operators

---

### 10.5 Digital Menu Optimization

With **42% of orders** starting on mobile:

- Apply same engineering principles to online menus
- Test different layouts and descriptions
- Update quickly based on performance
- Ensure mobile-friendly formatting

## 11. Conclusion

### Key Takeaways

1. **Menu engineering is essential**—it's a systematic way to maximize profitability without raising prices across the board.

2. **Use contribution margin, not food cost percentage**—the dollar value matters more than the percentage.

3. **The matrix reveals hidden opportunities**—Puzzles are often hiding in plain sight, waiting for visibility.

4. **Design matters**—the Golden Triangle, descriptive language, and strategic pricing can increase orders by 20-30%.

5. **It's a continuous discipline**—quarterly analysis keeps your menu aligned with costs and preferences.

6. **Technology simplifies the process**—modern POS and restaurant management software automate much of the heavy lifting.
