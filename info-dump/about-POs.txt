🧾 What Is a Restaurant PO?
A purchase order (PO) is a commercial document — the first official offer from a buyer to a seller. It includes item descriptions, quantities, agreed prices, delivery dates, and special instructions. Once signed by both parties, it becomes a binding agreement. 
Revolution Ordering
 Restaurants use POs for ingredients, equipment, cleaning supplies, and anything else needed to operate.

📅 How Frequently Do Restaurants Raise POs?
Frequency varies by category of item:

Category	Frequency
Fresh produce & dairy	2–3 times per week or daily
Meat & seafood	2–4 times per week
Dry goods & condiments	Weekly or bi-weekly
Cleaning supplies & disposables	Monthly or bulk
The number of orders placed per week varies based on sales volume and available storage space. Restaurants must figure out what works best for them — bulk ordering dry goods, while having seafood or fresh produce coming in every few days.

A common best practice is to create standing orders for consistent items ordered weekly at the same quantities, and schedule orders on the same days each week — for example, Monday for meats, Tuesday for produce.

📦 Quantities — How Much Do They Order?
Restaurants calculate par levels (minimum stock to keep on hand) using actual POS sales data over 4–6 weeks, then add a 20–30% safety buffer for unexpected busy periods, adjusting seasonally and reviewing quarterly.

Key targets restaurants aim for:

Perishables: 3–7 days of inventory on hand; inventory turnover of 4–12× annually (higher for fresh items)

Food cost percentage: 28–35% of sales is the industry target

Vendors have their own case sizes that differ significantly — for instance, the industry standard for a case of eggs is 30 dozen, but vendors range from 12 to 40 dozen. These differences directly impact food costs and storage.

🗂️ Per Item or Bulk? How Are POs Structured?
Restaurants use two main structures:

PO by Supplier: Orders are structured around individual suppliers, emphasizing the vendor relationship. For example, a seafood order goes specifically to the fish supplier with types and quantities listed. PO by Category/Ingredient: Orders are structured around food categories, potentially spanning multiple suppliers — like sourcing sirloin from Supplier A and ribeye from Supplier B.

In practice, a single PO typically covers multiple items from one supplier (bulk per vendor), not one PO per item. However, for specialty ingredients, separate POs per supplier are common.

Best practice is to maintain 2–3 vendors for main categories (meat, dairy, produce) and a handful for specialty items like cured meats, wines, or mushrooms.

🔄 The PO Process — Step by Step
The restaurant manager or chef identifies needed ingredients and supplies, creates a detailed list with quantities and specifications, and sends it to preferred suppliers as a PO. Suppliers review, confirm or suggest modifications, and then prepare the items for shipment.

The PO lists information for both parties — products ordered, amounts, bid price, extended price, and total. Multiple copies are prepared so the purchaser, supplier, accounting office, and receiving clerk all have records.

📊 Seasonal Adjustments
Restaurants increase par levels by 20–40% before busy seasons and stock extra popular items. During slow months, par levels are cut by 30–50%. Orders are placed 7–10 days before major holidays like Valentine's Day or Mother's Day to account for supplier lead times.

⚡ Key Takeaway
In summary: restaurants raise multiple POs per week (sometimes daily for perishables), typically grouped by supplier rather than one PO per item. Quantities are driven by par levels, storage capacity, and historical sales data — not guesswork. Large chains may also funnel everything through a central kitchen that then distributes to individual outlets.

Great follow-up! Shelf life and lead time are actually core drivers of how restaurants plan their POs. Here's a deep dive:

🕐 Lead Time — How Far in Advance Do Restaurants Order?
Lead time varies by supplier type and item category:

Supplier / Item Type	Typical Lead Time
Local produce vendors	1–2 days
Meat & seafood (local)	1–3 days
Dry goods / packaged items	3–7 days
Specialty / imported items	1–4 weeks
Equipment & non-food	2–6 weeks
Beverages & wines	1–2 weeks
The golden rule in restaurant procurement is: Order = (Daily Usage × Lead Time) + Safety Stock

So if a restaurant uses 10kg of chicken per day and lead time is 2 days, they need at least 20kg in reserve before placing the next order — plus a buffer.

📦 Shelf Life — How It Directly Shapes PO Quantity & Frequency
Shelf life essentially caps how much you can order at once. Ordering more than what can be consumed before expiry = direct loss.

Category	Shelf Life	Ordering Implication
Fresh fish / seafood	1–2 days	Order daily or every other day, tiny quantities
Fresh meat (raw)	3–5 days	2–3× per week, small batches
Fresh produce (leafy)	3–7 days	2–3× per week
Root vegetables	2–4 weeks	Weekly, larger quantities
Dairy (milk, cream)	5–10 days	Twice weekly
Cheese	2–6 weeks	Weekly to bi-weekly
Eggs	3–5 weeks	Weekly
Frozen items	3–12 months	Bi-weekly to monthly, bulk orders
Dry goods (flour, rice, pasta)	6–24 months	Monthly, bulk
Canned / packaged	1–3 years	Monthly or quarterly, high volume
⚖️ The Shelf Life vs. Lead Time Tension
This is where restaurants have to be really precise. The relationship looks like this:

Usable Window = Shelf Life − Lead Time − Buffer Days

Example (Fresh Cream):
Shelf life = 8 days
Lead time = 2 days
Buffer = 1 day
→ Usable window = 5 days worth of stock you can safely order
If a restaurant over-orders because lead time is short, but doesn't account for shelf life — the excess spoils. This is one of the top causes of food waste and cost overruns in restaurants.

🔁 How Shelf Life Determines PO Cycle
Restaurants typically set their reorder point so that new stock arrives just as old stock is about to run out:

Seafood: Near-zero buffer tolerance. Ordered fresh almost daily. PO quantities are tiny — often just enough for that day's expected covers.
Produce: 2–3 days buffer. POs raised 2–3 times a week.
Dairy: 3–4 days buffer. POs raised twice a week.
Dry goods: 7–10 day buffer comfortable. POs raised weekly or bi-weekly in bulk.
Frozen: Can hold for months — bulk orders, monthly POs. Ordered more for price advantage (bulk discounts).
🏪 FIFO — The Shelf Life Management Rule
All restaurants follow FIFO (First In, First Out) when receiving new PO stock:

New deliveries go to the back of the shelf
Older stock comes to the front for use first
This is critical for items with tight shelf lives like dairy and produce
Without FIFO discipline, even perfectly timed POs result in waste.

📉 Impact on PO Quantities — Summary Formula
Most restaurants calculate order quantity like this:

Order Quantity = (Daily Usage × Reorder Cycle Days) + Safety Stock − Current Inventory

Where:
Reorder Cycle Days ≤ Shelf Life (always)
Safety Stock = 1–2 days of usage for perishables
              = 5–7 days for dry goods
🔑 Key Insight
The shorter the shelf life, the more frequent but smaller the PO. The longer the shelf life, the less frequent but larger and more bulk-oriented the PO. Lead time acts as a hard deadline — if lead time is 3 days and shelf life is only 4 days, you're essentially operating with zero margin for error, which is why fresh seafood and specialty herbs are ordered almost daily in fine dining restaurants.

## ⏰ **REORDER TIME (When to Order)**

### The Reorder Point Formula
```
Reorder Point = (Daily Usage × Lead Time) + Safety Stock
```

| Component | What It Means |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Daily Usage** | Average consumption per day (from POS data, usually 4-6 weeks average) |
| **Lead Time** | Days from placing PO to delivery |
| **Safety Stock** | Buffer for unexpected demand or delays |

### Example Calculation
**Chicken Breast:**
- Daily usage: 15 kg/day
- Lead time: 2 days
- Safety stock: 5 kg (1 day buffer)

**Reorder Point = (15 × 2) + 5 = 35 kg**

→ **When inventory hits 35 kg, place the order**

---

## 📦 **REORDER AMOUNT (How Much to Order)**

### The Order Quantity Formula
```
Order Quantity = (Daily Usage × Reorder Cycle Days) + Safety Stock − Current Inventory
```

| Component | What It Means |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Reorder Cycle Days** | How many days until next delivery (constrained by shelf life) |
| **Safety Stock** | Buffer stock (1-2 days for perishables, 5-7 for dry goods) |
| **Current Inventory** | What's already in stock |

### Example Calculation
**Fresh Cream:**
- Daily usage: 5 liters/day
- Reorder cycle: 3 days (limited by 8-day shelf life minus lead time)
- Safety stock: 5 liters
- Current inventory: 8 liters

**Order Quantity = (5 × 3) + 5 − 8 = 12 liters**

## 🧮 **The Complete Working Model**

### Step-by-Step Process

| Step | Action | Calculation |
|------|--------|-------------|
| **1. Determine Daily Usage** | Average from POS data over 4-6 weeks | `Total sold ÷ Number of days` |
| **2. Set Safety Stock** | Buffer for variability | Perishables: 1-2 days usage<br>Dry goods: 5-7 days usage |
| **3. Confirm Lead Time** | Supplier's delivery commitment | 1-2 days (local) to 2-6 weeks (imported) |
| **4. Calculate Reorder Point** | Trigger level for new PO | `(Daily Usage × Lead Time) + Safety Stock` |
| **5. Set Reorder Cycle** | Max days between orders | `Shelf Life − Lead Time − 1 day buffer` |
| **6. Calculate Order Quantity** | How much to order this time | `(Daily Usage × Cycle Days) + Safety Stock − Current Stock` |

## ⚠️ **Key Constraints & Adjustments**

### The Shelf Life Ceiling
```
Reorder Cycle Days ≤ (Shelf Life − Lead Time − Buffer)
```

**Example — Fresh Herbs:**
- Shelf life: 4 days
- Lead time: 2 days  
- Buffer: 1 day
- **Max reorder cycle = 1 day** → Must order almost daily

### Seasonal Adjustments
| Scenario | Adjustment |
|----------|------------|
| **High season prep** | Increase par levels 20-40% |
| **Holiday ordering** | Place PO 7-10 days before (account for supplier lead times) |
| **Slow periods** | Reduce par levels 30-50% |
| **Price volatility** | Bulk up on shelf-stable items when prices drop |

## 🔧 **Pro Tips from the Industry**

| Best Practice | Why It Matters |
|-------------|--------------|
| **Standing orders** for consistent items | Reduces decision fatigue, ensures consistency |
| **Same-day ordering** (e.g., Mondays = meat, Tuesdays = produce) | Creates rhythm, easier supplier management |
| **FIFO discipline** | Prevents waste even with perfect PO timing |
| **Weekly PO review** | Adjust for changing demand patterns |
| **2-3 vendors per category** | Backup options if lead times stretch |

## 📊 **Quick Reference: The Golden Rules**

1. **Reorder Point** = Cover lead time + safety buffer
2. **Reorder Quantity** = Cover cycle time + safety − what's on hand
3. **Shelf life always wins** — never order more than you can use
4. **Shorter shelf life = more frequent, smaller POs**
5. **Longer shelf life = less frequent, bulk POs**
